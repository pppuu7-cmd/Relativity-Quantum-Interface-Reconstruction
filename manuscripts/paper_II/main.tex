\title{Relativity--Quantum Interface Reconstruction II: Nuisance-Profiled Statistical Identifiability in Calibration-Nullspace Observables}

\author{Author Name}
\affiliation{Institution, City, Country}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
We develop the statistical-identifiability layer of Relativity--Quantum Interface Reconstruction (RQIR) for observables that have already been projected into a calibration nullspace. Building on the deterministic observable construction of RQIR I, we formulate a covariance-whitened tangent-space geometry for a target deformation parameter $\beta$ in the presence of shared and branch-specific nuisance parameters. For a Gaussian mean model with parameter-independent covariance, we show that the nuisance-profiled Fisher information is the squared norm of the component of the whitened target tangent orthogonal to the nuisance tangent space. The same quantity is the generalized Schur complement of the nuisance Fisher block, with rank-deficient cases treated by the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse. This yields a precise local criterion for first-order statistical identifiability and cleanly separates three failure modes: removal by calibration, degeneracy with physical nuisance directions, and inadequate finite-data sensitivity after both projections. We further prove that enlarging the nuisance space cannot increase the profiled information and that profiling nuisance coefficients independently in separate branches is generally more conservative than a joint fit with genuinely shared nuisance parameters. Applying the framework to the current RQIR benchmark families, we find a sharp branch split: the signed/global benchmark has positive profiled information but extremely weak physical sensitivity under the locked normalization, whereas the positive/local benchmark lies at the Fisher-collapse boundary after the locked nuisance profile. The framework therefore turns the qualitative statement that a projected signal ``survives'' into a quantitative test of whether the deformation is statistically distinguishable and practically measurable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A detector-facing reconstruction problem can fail at more than one mathematical layer. A putative physical deformation may be removed by a calibration transformation, it may survive calibration but remain locally degenerate with other physical nuisance directions, or it may be statistically identifiable while being far too small to measure with a realistic data volume. These possibilities are often compressed into the single language of ``sensitivity,'' even though they correspond to different questions and require different diagnostics.

RQIR I established the first layer of this hierarchy for a multimessenger timing construction: after linearization of a three-channel response with unknown calibration gains, the retained scalar is obtained by projection into the left nullspace of the calibration tangent matrix. The result is an observable-level quantity with an explicitly propagated covariance and a benchmark deformation proportional to a prompt normalization, a cosmological kernel, and a source-frame spectral factor \cite{RQIRI}. That construction deliberately stopped before asking whether the deformation parameter can be separated from additional nuisance variations.

The present article addresses precisely that second question. We formulate statistical identifiability geometrically in the covariance-whitened data space and connect the geometry to the standard Fisher and profile-likelihood language \cite{Fisher1922,Rao1945,CoxReid1987,Cowan2011}. The central observation is simple but operationally important: once the calibration directions have been removed, the target tangent must still have a component outside the tangent space generated by all admissible nuisance parameters. Only that orthogonal component carries local information that cannot be mimicked by nuisance motion.

This perspective makes four contributions. First, it gives an invariant expression for the nuisance-profiled information as an orthogonal projection in whitened data space and proves its equivalence to a generalized Schur complement of the Fisher matrix. Second, it gives a first-order local-identifiability criterion that remains well defined when the nuisance Fisher block is rank deficient. Third, it proves monotonic information loss as the nuisance model is enlarged and clarifies the difference between genuinely shared nuisance parameters and branch-specific nuisance copies. Fourth, it applies these results to the two benchmark families currently retained in RQIR, exposing a physically important branch asymmetry: one benchmark remains locally identifiable but quantitatively weak, while the other collapses to zero profiled Fisher information under the locked nuisance model.

Throughout, we distinguish three statements:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}\text{algebraic observability}&\neq\text{statistical identifiability}\\ &\neq\text{finite-data detectability}.\end{aligned}
\label{eq:three_layers}
\end{equation}
The first is inherited from RQIR I. The second is the main subject of this work. The third requires a normalization, an event/exposure model, and a target precision. Keeping the layers separate prevents a formally nonzero signal from being mistaken for a realistic measurement forecast.

\section{Interface with RQIR I and scope}
\label{sec:interface}

Let $\bm y$ denote the original channel-level data vector and let $A$ be the calibration tangent matrix obtained by linearizing the detector response about a reference calibration. RQIR I constructs a left-null projection $Q$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
Q A = 0,
\end{equation}
so that the reduced observable
\begin{equation}
\bm d = Q\bm y
\end{equation}
is insensitive, to the retained linear order, to the calibration directions represented by $A$. If the covariance of $\bm y$ is $\Sigma_y$, the reduced covariance is
\begin{equation}
C = Q\Sigma_y Q^{T}.
\label{eq:projected_cov}
\end{equation}
The statistical analysis below begins from $(\bm d,C)$; it does not reopen the detector-calibration problem.

This ordering matters. Calibration-null projection and nuisance profiling are different operations acting on different tangent spaces. The former defines which combination of raw channels is treated as observable at the adopted calibration order. The latter asks whether the target deformation can be distinguished, within that observable space, from the remaining physical or phenomenological degrees of freedom.

We work primarily with a local Gaussian mean model,
\begin{equation}
p(\bm d\mid\beta,\bm\eta)
=\mathcal{N}\!\left[\bm\mu(\beta,\bm\eta),C\right],
\label{eq:gaussian_model}
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is the scalar parameter of interest and $\bm\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$ is a nuisance vector. Unless stated otherwise, $C$ is evaluated at the audit point and treated as parameter independent. This assumption isolates the geometry relevant to the current RQIR implementation. Parameter-dependent covariance contributes additional Fisher terms and is deferred to a later extension.

The results are local: they describe the linearized tangent geometry at a specified point $(\beta_0,\bm\eta_0)$. A positive Fisher diagnostic is therefore a statement of first-order local identifiability, not a proof of global identifiability of the nonlinear likelihood.

\section{Covariance-whitened tangent geometry}
\label{sec:geometry}

For positive-definite $C$, define the whitened target tangent
\begin{equation}
\bm g
=C^{-1/2}\,\partial_{\beta}\bm\mu
\label{eq:gdef}
\end{equation}
and the whitened nuisance tangent matrix
\begin{equation}
W
=C^{-1/2}
\begin{bmatrix}
\partial_{\eta_1}\bm\mu & \cdots & \partial_{\eta_m}\bm\mu
\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:Wdef}
\end{equation}
All derivatives in this article are evaluated at the audit point unless an argument is displayed explicitly.

The nuisance tangent space is
\begin{equation}
\Nuis=\Col(W).
\end{equation}
Because nuisance columns may be linearly dependent, we use the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse \cite{Penrose1955}. The orthogonal projector onto $\Nuis$ is
\begin{equation}
\proj_{\Nuis}
=W(W^{T}W)^{+}W^{T},
\label{eq:nuisproj}
\end{equation}
and the target component that cannot be generated by an infinitesimal nuisance displacement is
\begin{equation}
\bm g_{\perp}
=(\Id-\proj_{\Nuis})\bm g.
\label{eq:gperp}
\end{equation}
This quantity is the central geometric object of the paper.

Whitening is not cosmetic. In unwhitened coordinates, Euclidean orthogonality has no direct likelihood meaning when measurement errors are correlated or heteroscedastic. Multiplication by $C^{-1/2}$ converts the covariance metric into the Euclidean metric, so a squared norm in whitened space coincides with the local Gaussian information metric.

\section{Nuisance-profiled Fisher information}
\label{sec:fisher}

For the Gaussian mean model in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gaussian_model}, the Fisher matrix for $\bm\theta=(\beta,\bm\eta)$ is
\begin{equation}
F_{ab}
=(\partial_a\bm\mu)^T C^{-1}(\partial_b\bm\mu).
\label{eq:fisher}
\end{equation}
In terms of $\bm g$ and $W$,
\begin{equation}
F=
\begin{pmatrix}
\bm g^T\bm g & \bm g^T W\\
W^T\bm g & W^T W
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:fisher_block}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Projection--Schur equivalence}

\textit{Proposition 1 (projection--Schur equivalence).} For the local model above, including rank-deficient nuisance tangent matrices,
\begin{align}
\Fprof_{\beta\beta}
&\equiv
\bm g^T(\Id-\proj_{\Nuis})\bm g
\label{eq:profiled_projection}\\
&=F_{\beta\beta}
-F_{\beta\eta}F_{\eta\eta}^{+}F_{\eta\beta}.
\label{eq:profiled_schur}
\end{align}

\textit{Proof.} Substitution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:nuisproj} into Eq.~\eqref{eq:profiled_projection} gives
\begin{equation}
\bm g^T\bm g
-\bm g^T W(W^TW)^+W^T\bm g.
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\eqref{eq:fisher_block}, the first term is $F_{\beta\beta}$, the two cross factors are $F_{\beta\eta}$ and $F_{\eta\beta}$, and $W^TW=F_{\eta\eta}$. This yields Eq.~\eqref{eq:profiled_schur}. The Moore--Penrose construction makes $W(W^TW)^+W^T$ the orthogonal projector onto $\Col(W)$ even if the nuisance columns are linearly dependent. \hfill$\square$

Equation~\eqref{eq:profiled_schur} is the generalized Schur complement associated with profiling nuisance directions. The projection form is more transparent geometrically; the block-Fisher form is convenient numerically and connects directly to conventional profile-likelihood calculations.

\subsection{Local first-order identifiability}

\textit{Proposition 2 (local tangent identifiability).} The target parameter is locally identifiable to first order against the nuisance model if and only if
\begin{equation}
\Fprof_{\beta\beta}>0,
\end{equation}
which is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
\bm g\notin\Col(W).
\label{eq:criterion}
\end{equation}

\textit{Proof.} Since $(\Id-\proj_{\Nuis})$ is an orthogonal projector,
\begin{equation}
\Fprof_{\beta\beta}
=\| (\Id-\proj_{\Nuis})\bm g\|_2^2\ge0.
\end{equation}
It vanishes exactly when $(\Id-\proj_{\Nuis})\bm g=0$, i.e., when $\bm g\in\Col(W)$. If the orthogonal component is nonzero, no first-order nuisance displacement reproduces the target tangent. \hfill$\square$

The qualifier ``to first order'' is essential. Higher-order curvature of the model manifold can, in principle, break a tangent degeneracy or introduce global aliases even when the local tangent is nondegenerate. Equation~\eqref{eq:criterion} is therefore a local structural diagnostic rather than a global theorem about the entire parameter space.

A useful dimensionless measure is the information-retention fraction
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\beta}
=\frac{\Fprof_{\beta\beta}}{F_{\beta\beta}},
\qquad F_{\beta\beta}>0.
\label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
Because an orthogonal projection cannot increase a norm,
\begin{equation}
0\le \rho_{\beta}\le1.
\end{equation}
Thus $\rho_{\beta}=1$ means that the nuisance model removes none of the local target information, while $\rho_{\beta}=0$ marks complete first-order degeneracy.

\subsection{Monotonicity under nuisance enrichment}

\textit{Proposition 3 (information monotonicity).} Let $\Nuis_1\subseteq\Nuis_2$ be two nuisance tangent spaces. Then
\begin{equation}
\Fprof_{\beta\beta}(\Nuis_2)
\le
\Fprof_{\beta\beta}(\Nuis_1).
\label{eq:monotonicity}
\end{equation}

\textit{Proof.} The distance from a fixed vector to a larger subspace cannot exceed the distance to a contained subspace. Since $\Fprof_{\beta\beta}(\Nuis)$ is the squared Euclidean distance from $\bm g$ to $\Nuis$ in whitened space, Eq.~\eqref{eq:monotonicity} follows. \hfill$\square$

This proposition is also a regression test. If a numerical pipeline reports an increase in profiled Fisher information after adding an unconstrained nuisance direction, either the nuisance spaces are not nested as assumed, additional prior information has been introduced, or the implementation is inconsistent.

\section{Shared and branch-specific nuisance parameters}
\label{sec:branches}

RQIR uses multiple physical benchmark branches to audit which conclusions are branch dependent. Combining branches raises a subtle issue: some nuisance parameters may be physically shared, while others may be specific to one branch or data subset. The distinction changes the nuisance tangent space and therefore the information retained on $\beta$.

Suppose statistically independent branches $b=1,\ldots,B$ contribute whitened target tangents $\bm g_b$ and nuisance matrices $W_b$. If the nuisance coefficient vector is genuinely shared across branches, the joint tangent objects are the stacked quantities
\begin{equation}
\bm g=\begin{bmatrix}\bm g_1\\ \vdots\\ \bm g_B\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
W=\begin{bmatrix}W_1\\ \vdots\\ W_B\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The joint profiled information is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}_{\rm joint}
=\bm g^T(\Id-\proj_{\Col(W)})\bm g.
\label{eq:joint}
\end{equation}

If, instead, the nuisance coefficients are allowed to vary independently by branch, the nuisance matrix becomes block diagonal,
\begin{equation}
W_{\rm sep}=\operatorname{diag}(W_1,\ldots,W_B),
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}_{\rm sep}
=\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\bm g_b^T(\Id-\proj_{\Col(W_b)})\bm g_b.
\label{eq:sep}
\end{equation}

\textit{Proposition 4 (shared-nuisance information bound).} If the branchwise model differs from the shared-nuisance model only by allowing independent copies of the same nuisance coefficients, then
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}_{\rm joint}\ge\mathcal{I}_{\rm sep}.
\label{eq:shared_bound}
\end{equation}

\textit{Proof.} Every shared nuisance displacement $(W_1\bm a,\ldots,W_B\bm a)$ is contained in the larger set of independent branch displacements $(W_1\bm a_1,\ldots,W_B\bm a_B)$. Hence $\Col(W)\subseteq\Col(W_{\rm sep})$. Proposition 3 then gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:shared_bound}. \hfill$\square$

Equality is not generic. It requires the nuisance compensation that best approximates the target tangent to be compatible with a common coefficient vector across branches. The result is useful in both directions: duplicating a physically shared nuisance parameter can erase legitimate cross-branch information, while incorrectly forcing a branch-specific systematic to be shared can produce overoptimistic sensitivity.

\section{RQIR benchmark families}
\label{sec:rqir_models}

The present numerical audit uses the branch definitions already locked in the RQIR repository. Their purpose here is not to claim a unique microscopic theory, but to expose how the statistical geometry behaves under two different reduced physical mappings.

\subsection{Signed/global benchmark}

The signed/global quantitative family is written as
\begin{equation}
\kappa_{\rm eff}
=\kappa_{\star}
\left(\frac{E_{\rm src}}{10^{19}\,{\rm eV}}\right)^p
(1+z)^{\gamma},
\label{eq:kappa_eff}
\end{equation}
with timing signal
\begin{equation}
\delta t_{\rm phys}
=\beta L_{\rm ref}{\cal N}_0\kappa_{\rm eff}M.
\label{eq:signed_signal}
\end{equation}
The residual nonlinear nuisance vector in the locked quantitative profile is
\begin{equation}
\bm\eta=(p,\gamma),
\end{equation}
with audit point
\begin{equation}
(\beta,p,\gamma)=(1,1,1).
\end{equation}
An additional amplitude-like nuisance is not included in this benchmark profile because an earlier local audit found it to be essentially degenerate with the target amplitude. The present branch should therefore be read as a conditional structural benchmark with a specified nuisance set, not as a universal forecast against every possible normalization uncertainty.

\subsection{Positive/local benchmark}

The positive/local family retains a more flexible local deformation,
\begin{multline}
\delta t_{\rm phys}
=A_{\rm ref}W_E(x)W_z(z)
(1+qx+q_2x^2)\\
\times(1+s_1z+s_2z^2),
\label{eq:positive_signal}
\end{multline}
with audit variables
\begin{equation}
\bm\vartheta=
(A_{\rm ref},\epsilon_-,\epsilon_+,q,q_2,s_1,s_2).
\end{equation}
The same locked nuisance-prior profile used in the repository audit is adopted here. This benchmark is deliberately more permissive locally; the statistical question is whether the target deformation retains any tangent component outside that enlarged nuisance model.

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}_{\beta}^{\rm signed/global}
\simeq9.645289993133135\times10^{-30},
\end{equation}
so the local Fisher scale is
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\beta}
\simeq\mathcal{I}_{\beta}^{-1/2}
\simeq3.220\times10^{14}.
\label{eq:signed_sigma}
\end{equation}
For a target precision $\sigma_{\beta}^{\star}=0.1$, the same linear exposure scaling implies
\begin{equation}
N_{\rm eff,req}^{\rm signed/global}
\simeq1.0367796180388115\times10^{33}.
\label{eq:signed_neff}
\end{equation}
Relative to the baseline $N_{\rm eff}=100$, this is an improvement factor of approximately $1.04\times10^{31}$.

The interpretation is deliberately two-part. Structurally, the branch passes the local identifiability test because the profiled information is positive. Quantitatively, the benchmark normalization is nowhere near a useful precision regime. The former statement is about tangent geometry; the latter is about the physical scale chosen for the current audit.

\subsection{Positive/local: Fisher collapse}

For the positive/local branch, the locked full profile produces
\begin{equation}
\Fprof_{\beta\beta,\,\rm local}
\simeq -2.2737367544323206\times10^{-13}
\end{equation}
in floating-point arithmetic. A negative Fisher information has no physical meaning here; the value is roundoff around the exact projection boundary. We therefore apply the nonnegativity implied by Eq.~\eqref{eq:profiled_projection} and set
\begin{equation}
\Fprof_{\beta\beta,\,\rm local}=0.
\label{eq:local_zero}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\beta}=\infty,
\qquad
N_{\rm eff,req}=\infty
\end{equation}
within the present local nuisance model. No multiplicative increase in event count can repair an exact first-order degeneracy: increasing exposure multiplies zero by a larger number, but does not rotate the target tangent out of the nuisance span.

This corrects a potentially misleading interpretation of prior-constrained behavior. Under the locked profile used here, the positive/local branch should not be described as ``prior rescued.'' It remains at the Fisher-collapse boundary. Breaking this degeneracy requires genuinely new information: a different observable, a physically justified constraint that changes the tangent geometry, or a nonlinear/higher-order effect demonstrated beyond the local Fisher approximation.

\section{From identifiability to detectability}
\label{sec:detectability}

If statistically independent events contribute identical expected information $\mathcal{I}_1$, then
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}_N=N\mathcal{I}_1
\end{equation}
and the Cram\'er--Rao scaling gives
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\beta}\gtrsim\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\mathcal{I}_1}}
\label{eq:cr_scaling}
\end{equation}
under the usual regularity and local-asymptotic assumptions \cite{Cramer1946,Rao1945}. For nonidentical events the sum generalizes to $\sum_i\mathcal{I}_i$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:cr_scaling} shows why identifiability and detectability must not be conflated. If $\mathcal{I}_1>0$ but extremely small, arbitrarily large exposure eventually reduces the formal Fisher uncertainty, yet the required data volume may be physically meaningless. If $\mathcal{I}_1=0$ because of an exact local degeneracy, exposure scaling alone cannot help at all.

For design studies it is therefore useful to report at least three numbers together: the unprofiled target information $F_{\beta\beta}$, the retention fraction $\rho_\beta$, and the physically normalized profiled information $\Fprof_{\beta\beta}$. The first quantifies the raw tangent amplitude, the second isolates loss to nuisance geometry, and the third determines the local statistical scale after both effects are combined.

\section{Reproducibility checks and limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The branch-specific arithmetic used in Table~\ref{tab:branch_results} is guarded in the RQIR repository by \nolinkurl{tests/test_branch_specific_physical_fisher_rates.py}, which currently reports 48 passing tests in the locked audit. The tests check the branch artifacts, the Fisher-rate arithmetic, the event-count inversion, and the explicit interpretation of the positive/local branch as zero information after clipping numerical roundoff.

There is, however, a reproducibility boundary that must remain visible in the manuscript. The historical Nim programs used for the full nonlinear profile were not re-executed in the present container because the Nim runtime was unavailable there. The quoted physical Fisher numbers are therefore analytically auditable from the locked artifact equations and protected by the Python regression guard, but an independent end-to-end rerun of the original nonlinear profile remains a pre-submission reproducibility task. We do not treat this runtime limitation as evidence for or against the physical result; it is simply a distinction between reproducing the locked arithmetic and rerunning the complete computational path that generated the locked coefficients.

Several theoretical limitations also define the scope of the present claims. First, the Fisher analysis is local and cannot exclude nonlinear degeneracies far from the audit point. Second, the baseline derivation treats the covariance as parameter independent; if $C=C(\theta)$, the Gaussian Fisher matrix acquires the familiar trace term
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{2}\Tr\!\left[C^{-1}(\partial_a C)C^{-1}(\partial_b C)\right],
\end{equation}
which changes the information geometry. Third, priors can change posterior precision but should not be rhetorically confused with information supplied by the likelihood. Fourth, the extremely weak signed/global sensitivity is conditional on the current normalization and event-weight model; changing those physical inputs can change the numerical forecast without changing Propositions 1--4. Finally, the branch comparison is diagnostic rather than a model-selection probability: the present calculation states how much local information each specified benchmark retains, not the posterior probability that one benchmark is the true microscopic description.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The geometric formulation exposes a hierarchy that is useful beyond the particular RQIR benchmark. The calibration-null construction defines an observable quotient: directions identified as detector-calibration freedoms are removed before physical inference. The nuisance-profiled Fisher construction then defines a second quotient at the tangent level: among variations that remain observable, it removes directions that can be reproduced by admissible nuisance motion. Finally, physical normalization and exposure determine whether the surviving tangent component is large enough to matter experimentally.

This hierarchy explains why the two current branches behave so differently. The signed/global benchmark retains a target component outside the two-dimensional $(p,\gamma)$ nuisance span. Its difficulty is not an exact tangent degeneracy but an exceedingly small physical information rate under the locked normalization. The positive/local benchmark, by contrast, is sufficiently flexible that the target tangent is absorbed by the locked nuisance model at the audit point. These are different negative outcomes and call for different remedies. The signed/global branch motivates better normalization, observables, or event leverage; the positive/local branch requires a change in identifiability geometry.

The shared-nuisance result also has practical consequences for future multi-branch or multi-instrument analyses. Treating every data subset as if it had an independent nuisance coefficient is conservative only when that independence is physically justified. If a parameter is truly common, tying it across data subsets can recover information through the incompatibility of a single nuisance displacement with all target responses simultaneously. Conversely, enforcing commonality without a physical basis can underestimate systematic uncertainty. The correct tangent space must therefore follow the causal and instrumental interpretation of the nuisance parameter, not merely computational convenience.

The next statistical step is to move beyond a single audit point. Maps of $\rho_\beta$ and $\Fprof_{\beta\beta}$ over redshift, source-frame energy, and branch hyperparameters can identify where the target tangent is naturally most transverse to nuisance directions. Such maps turn the present diagnostic into an experimental-design tool: the goal is not simply to maximize raw signal amplitude, but to maximize the part of that amplitude that remains orthogonal to nuisance variations in the covariance metric.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have constructed the nuisance-profiled statistical layer of RQIR on top of the calibration-null observable defined in RQIR I. The principal results are geometric and independent of the detailed benchmark normalization. For a Gaussian mean model with fixed covariance, the local information on a scalar target parameter after profiling nuisance directions is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\Fprof_{\beta\beta}
=\left\|(\Id-\proj_{\Nuis})\bm g\right\|_2^2
=F_{\beta\beta}-F_{\beta\eta}F_{\eta\eta}^{+}F_{\eta\beta}.
}
\end{equation}
It is positive exactly when the whitened target tangent has a component outside the nuisance tangent space. Enlarging that nuisance space cannot increase the retained information, and allowing independent nuisance copies in separate branches cannot yield more information than a fit in which the parameter is genuinely shared.

The current RQIR audit then separates two qualitatively different outcomes. The signed/global branch is locally identifiable after profiling $(p,\gamma)$ but has a physical Fisher rate so small that the locked benchmark would require an extreme effective event count to reach $\sigma_\beta=0.1$. The positive/local branch instead sits at the local Fisher-collapse boundary under its locked nuisance profile and cannot be repaired by exposure alone. The two branches must therefore not be merged into a single statement of ``sensitivity.''

The broader methodological conclusion is that survival of calibration projection is necessary but not sufficient for inference. A defensible reconstruction must report what survives calibration, what survives nuisance profiling, and what remains measurable after physical normalization. RQIR II supplies the second of these layers and establishes the diagnostics needed to connect the observable construction of RQIR I to branch-aware likelihood analyses and future identifiability maps.
